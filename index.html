Rule 16.1 is the first Federal Rule of Civil Procedure written for multidistrict litigation, a part of the docket that had been running without one. The Judicial Panel reported 158 MDL proceedings pending at the end of 2025, holding more than 340,000 actions by one industry count. Until December 1, 2025, how a transferee judge opened one of those proceedings was governed by the Manual for Complex Litigation, which is Federal Judicial Center guidance rather than law.

What it does. The transferee court should order the parties to meet and submit a report, should hold an initial management conference, and should enter an initial management order after it. The Rule lists 18 subjects that report must cover.

Why count it. None of that binds the judge. The Rule is written in "should," not "shall," and its mandatory language runs to the parties' report rather than to the court. Six weeks in, K&L Gates observed that Rule 16.1 "does not impose mandatory obligations on transferee courts" and that its "effectiveness will ultimately depend on whether the transferee court chooses to enforce its provisions." That makes its effect an empirical question. This dataset measures it by reading every initial management order in every MDL created since the effective date.

Quotations from K&L Gates (Jan. 6, 2026), checked against the source. Crowell & Moring (Dec. 1, 2025) and Sidley Austin (Feb. 19, 2026) each described variation among courts without naming one or giving a figure. Pending-MDL count: JPML, Calendar Year Statistics (Dec. 31, 2025); consolidated-action count: K&L Gates, above.

Findings

  1. 7 of the 14 readable orders cite Rule 16.1 by name; 7 do not. For a rule that binds the judge to nothing, that is the threshold question, and 8 months in the answer is half. The subject matter is not being skipped: all 14 call for a report and address leadership counsel. But citing orders go further, addressing a median of 19 of the 19 items tracked here against 11 for the orders that do not cite it. Both groups spread, 18 to 19 and 4 to 14, so the medians describe the centre rather than every order. 2 non-citing orders designate the Manual for Complex Litigation instead. The likelier reading is not that courts ignored Rule 16.1 but that it has not displaced the Manual.
  2. Citing orders put more subjects in play and decide fewer of them. The figure above counts every item an order raises, including items swept in by a clause incorporating Rule 16.1(b) wholesale. Count only what an order names in its own words and the medians are 18 and 11, and the citing spread widens to 1 at the low end, because two courts reach the Rule's subjects entirely by reference and name almost none of them. Count only what a court actually settles, such that the parties cannot change it in their report, and the ranking inverts: citing orders decide 16% of the subjects they raise, against 28% for orders that never mention the Rule. Breadth and depth run in opposite directions, and any coverage figure has to say which one it means.
  3. Two items are addressed by every order: whether leadership counsel should be appointed and the procedure for selecting them. Both stand at 14 of 14 orders read subject by subject, Appointing leadership is long-standing MDL practice, not something Rule 16.1 introduced, so unanimity here reads as the Rule tracking settled practice, not changing it. The least-addressed items are periodic review of leadership appointments and measures to facilitate resolution, 7 of 14 each.
  4. Direct filing is managed far more often than it is named. An earlier pass over the order-level summary counted it among the least-addressed items. Reading the full texts moves it to 11 of 14: orders that never put direct filing on an agenda still settle it, through clauses consolidating later-filed actions automatically. One court goes further and invites direct filing outright, prescribing a cover-sheet notation and the filing fee.
  5. A court has applied Rule 16.1 to an MDL centralized before the Rule existed. Practitioner commentary is divided on whether the Rule reaches MDLs centralized before December 1, 2025. It does, at least in one court's hands. In re Change Healthcare, Inc., Customer Data Security Breach Litigation, MDL No. 3108 (D. Minn.), was centralized in June 2024. Pretrial Order No. 28, entered March 19, 2026, puts eight items on a status conference agenda "[p]ursuant to the Court's Pretrial Order No. 2 … and consistent with Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 16.1 aimed at providing case-management guidance in MDLs." Read the verbs. "Pursuant to" is spent on the court's own earlier order; the Rule gets "consistent with." The court aligns itself with Rule 16.1 rather than holding that the Rule governs, which is a narrower move than either side of the commentary anticipated. Parties have gone further: in In re Uber Technologies, Inc., Passenger Sexual Assault Litigation, MDL No. 3084 (N.D. Cal.), centralized in October 2023, both sides briefed a discovery dispute out of the Rule 16.1 advisory committee notes in April 2026, and a January 2026 brief argued that a proposed pleading restriction was "incompatible with" the Rule. Until August 13, 2026 this site published the opposite finding. The search that produced it could not have found these orders; see the next item.
  6. The search that established the old null excluded, by construction, every case that could have refuted it. The sweep filtered on filed_after, which in this index restricts by the date the case was filed, not the date the document was filed. A pre-Rule MDL is a case filed before the effective date, so the query removed the entire population it was meant to search, returned nothing, and the nothing was read as an answer. Rerun with the document-level filter, the same seven forms return 101 documents rather than 68, an increase of 33. One form went from zero hits to eight. All 33 additions have now been read. Thirteen contain no literal "16.1" anywhere and are ordinary Rule 16 filings the index returned because it does not treat 16.1 as a token distinct from 16. Seven refer only to a local rule numbered 16.1. The remaining thirteen are real invocations of the federal Rule, and they include the orders that overturned the null. The filter was not misused; it was the wrong filter with a plausible name, and every published count that rested on it was wrong in the same direction. A null needs a demonstration that the search could have returned a counterexample, and this one never had it.
  7. Some of what a search returns is the clerk's text, not the filing's. The index covers the docket entry description alongside the document's own text, which this project had assumed it did not. Nine of the 101 results have no readable text at all, six of them filings in a single bankruptcy adversary proceeding; every occurrence of the Rule's name in those sits in the clerk's entry. The effect cuts both ways. It is good for coverage, because an order that exists only as docket text is findable after all. It is bad for interpretation, because a hit may be a clerk's summary rather than anything a party or a judge wrote, and those are different kinds of evidence. The dataset now records which one each hit is.
  8. Counting uptake by search undercounts it, and the forms almost never overlap. Five of the seven forms returned something no other form did. Across 101 documents exactly one names the Rule more than one way: the Rule 16.1 report filed in MDL 3170, whose thirteen pages use both the abbreviation and the phrase "Rule 16.1 Report." It matched a third form only because the clerk docketed it as "STATUS Report F.R.C.P. 16.1," a spelling that appears nowhere in the filing. Every other filing commits to a single form, so a search on any one of them sees a near-strict subset of the whole. Two of the seven forms are not distinct queries at all, since the index normalizes internal spacing but not periods inside an acronym. A study of this Rule that searched one way would have found part of the record and had no way to know what it missed.
  9. The Rule is being cited in cases it does not govern. Six orders across two districts, filed between February 2026 and July 2026, cite the Rule in ordinary civil cases that involve no transfer, no consolidation, and no class. They divide into two kinds. Four are District of New Jersey scheduling orders from one magistrate judge, citing "Fed. R. Civ. P. 16.1 and L. Civ. R. 16.1(a)" as authority for an initial conference; one is captioned "Letter Order Pursuant to Rule 16.1." That district has its own Local Civil Rule 16.1 on scheduling conferences, cited correctly in the same sentence. The other two are District of Nevada discovery orders reciting counsel's stipulation that the parties made "initial and supplemental disclosures pursuant to FRCP 16.1," a proposition governed by Rule 26(a)(1), about which Rule 16.1 says nothing. Rule 16.1(a) operates after the Panel transfers actions. One pattern comes from a court and the other from counsel, and the numbering that makes this Rule hard to search is implicated in both. A third pattern has since appeared on the party side, in two districts more. A motion in a 2021 civil case in the District of North Dakota asks for a status conference citing "Local Civil Rule 16.1; Fed. R. Civ. P. 16.1(a)," then three pages later cites the same local rule alongside "Fed. R. Civ. P. 16(a)(1), (2)," which is right. One brief, both versions, which is the numbering collision happening inside a single document. In a Florida bankruptcy adversary proceeding, a scheduling report, a motion, a response and a hearing notice are all captioned under "F.R.C.P. 16.1," one of them citing "F.R.C.P. 16.1(2)(D)," a subdivision the federal Rule does not have. Six court orders remains a floor rather than a count: it is what a search on seven spellings of one rule name could find.
  10. No order has addressed disclosure of third-party litigation funding, 0 of 14. The Rule does not require the subject to be raised; (b)(4) lets the parties raise it and none has. The Advisory Committee has a subcommittee studying funding disclosure, and this is the practice figure that question otherwise lacks.
  11. The interval from transfer to the initial conference runs from 21 days to 198. Rule 16.1(a) says the court "should schedule an initial management conference" and sets no deadline. The ninefold spread across the 12 orders that set a date is that discretion expressed in days.
  12. Where the Rule is applied, it appears to travel as a form order. Judge Quraishi's Initial Procedure Order No. 1 in the Dupixent MDL (D.N.J., June 2026) is the same form order Judge Bates entered in D.D.C. five months earlier, including 3 added items appearing verbatim in both and in neither the Rule nor the Committee Note. The clearest evidence is a gap rather than a similarity: the D.N.J. order runs from paragraph 8 to paragraph 10, with no paragraph 9. In the D.D.C. order, paragraph 9 waives a local rule on class certification that has no D.N.J. counterpart. It was cut from the form and the rest was never renumbered. If the pattern holds, what moves between districts is one judge's order, not the Rule text.
  13. Search biases the uptake rate upward and the invocation count downward, for different reasons. The first 3 orders examined here all cited the Rule, an apparent 100%. They were found by searching for the phrase, and such a search returns only documents that use it; enumerating the Panel's full list produced 50%. Selecting cases by the phrase inflates the rate. But as the finding above records, searching a single form of the Rule's name deflates the count, because the naming forms are mutually exclusive. Both are limits on the method rather than facts about this Rule, and they pull in opposite directions, so neither corrects the other.
  14. Parties have already departed from the Rule's single-report structure. Rule 16.1(b)(1) contemplates that the parties meet and submit one report; the Rule's mandatory language then governs what that report must contain. In the Trans Union MDL, one plaintiff group filed its own Rule 16.1 report separately, 5 weeks after the effective date.

19 columns cover the Rule's 18 listed items: this dataset splits (b)(2)(A)(iii) into selection of leadership and periodic review of it.

Rule 16.1

The 18 required subjects break down as 7 on leadership counsel; 4 on scheduling, direct filing, and related actions; and 7 on the parties' initial views. Subdivision (b)(4) permits anything else but requires nothing, which is why litigation funding appears here as a null rather than as a violation.

Full text of Rule 16.1

(a) Initial Management Conference. After the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation transfers actions, the transferee court should schedule an initial management conference to develop an initial plan for orderly pretrial activity in the MDL proceedings.

(b) Report for the Conference.

(1) Submitting a Report. The transferee court should order the parties to meet and to submit a report to the court before the conference.

(2) Required Content: the Parties' Views on Leadership Counsel and Other Matters. The report must address any matter the court designates—which may include any matter in Rule 16—and, unless the court orders otherwise, the parties' views on:

(A) whether leadership counsel should be appointed and, if so:

(i) the timing of the appointments;

(ii) the structure of leadership counsel;

(iii) the procedure for selecting leadership and whether the appointments should be reviewed periodically;

(iv) their responsibilities and authority in conducting pretrial activities and any role in facilitating resolution of the MDL proceedings;

(v) the proposed methods for regularly communicating with and reporting to the court and nonleadership counsel;

(vi) any limits on activity by nonleadership counsel; and

(vii) whether and when to establish a means for compensating leadership counsel;

(B) any previously entered scheduling or other orders that should be vacated or modified;

(C) a schedule for additional management conferences with the court;

(D) how to manage the direct filing of new actions in the MDL proceedings; and

(E) whether related actions have been—or are expected to—be filed in other courts, and whether to adopt methods for coordinating with them.

(3) Additional Required Content: the Parties' Initial Views on Various Matters. Unless the court orders otherwise, the report also must address the parties' initial views on:

(A) whether consolidated pleadings should be prepared;

(B) how and when the parties will exchange information about the factual bases for their claims and defenses;

(C) discovery, including any difficult issues that may arise;

(D) any likely pretrial motions;

(E) whether the court should consider any measures to facilitate resolving some or all actions before the court;

(F) whether any matters should be referred to a magistrate judge or a master; and

(G) the principal factual and legal issues likely to be presented.

(4) Permitted Content. The report may include any other matter that the parties wish to bring to the court's attention.

(c) Initial Management Order. After the conference, the court should enter an initial management order addressing the matters in Rule 16.1(b) and, in the court's discretion, any other matters. This order controls the course of the proceedings unless the court modifies it.

Quoted verbatim, including the Rule's own punctuation. Text as published by the Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School, law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_16.1, retrieved 12 August 2026. Confirm against the official text before quoting the Rule in a filing.

Method and limitations

Every figure here is a floor. The universe comes from the Judicial Panel's monthly list rather than from a search, so it includes MDLs whose orders never use the phrase "Rule 16.1." Each transferee court's first management order is read in full and coded from the order text, with a pin cite to the paragraph. Coverage is 14 of 16: one order exists and has not been read (MDL 3187, no text layer on any of its four docketed orders), and one MDL has issued no qualifying order at all (3176), which is a fact about that proceeding rather than a gap. The two are never merged.

Coding conventions and the rest of the limitations

Three conventions govern the coding.

1. Code the document, not descriptions of it. Not a party's characterization of an order, not a firm alert, not the docket entry.

2. Silence is recorded as not addressed, never as a refusal. Only an affirmative declination is coded as one.

3. Ambiguity is recorded as unclear, with the language quoted rather than resolved.

Sources link to free copies on CourtListener or on the court's own site; none requires a PACER account. The full codebook is in PROTOCOL.md; the known problems are in AUDIT.md. Corrections are welcome here and are recorded in the changelog.

Limitations.

The pin cites are two-tiered. 6 rows (3162, 3163, 3166, 3171, 3174, 3175) give paragraph pointers without quoted language; the other 8 quote it directly. Those 6 were coded earliest, and 4 of the 7 citing orders sit among them, which means the citation count currently rests most heavily on the rows with the thinnest documentation. They are being back-filled.

Initial orders only, with a growing set of exceptions: a later order may invoke the Rule where the first did not, as has happened in MDL 3175 and in MDL 3163, whose Case Management Order No. 12 of July 2, 2026 cites Rule 16.1(b)(3)(B)'s committee note in support of early exchange on cross-cutting issues while its initial order never mentions the Rule. Those later orders are recorded in the invocation table, not in the order-level coding, so the citation rate on this page is a rate for first orders.

The search sweep is a floor, and was recently a badly understated one. Full-text search reaches only what RECAP holds and only what has a text layer; the four docketed orders in MDL 3187, both copies of the plaintiffs' report in MDL 3175, and the initial order in MDL 3180 have none, so no query of any kind can return them. The MDL 3180 order is in this dataset only because a copy was obtained outside RECAP. Until August 13, 2026 the sweep also ran under a filter that restricted by case filing date rather than document filing date, which hid 33 documents and produced a published null that was false. The corrected sweep returns 101 documents, all of which have now been read individually. A further limit surfaced in that reading: the index covers the clerk's docket text as well as the filing's own, so some results are entries rather than documents, and the search log records which.

Reports routed to chambers are invisible. 2 of the 14 courts direct counsel to email the report rather than file it, one stating it "should not be filed." No docket-based method, including this one, can observe those.

One coder, no second rater, and so no inter-rater statistic. Every entry carries a pin cite so that disagreement is with a quotation.

N is small and the Rule is 8 months old. Nothing here supports a claim about settled practice.
