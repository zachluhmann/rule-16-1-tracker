Every law firm alert on Rule 16.1 says the same thing: courts are handling it differently. None of them says how differently, or which courts. This site is the count. It covers every MDL the Judicial Panel has created since the Rule took effect, and it reads each judge's first management order against every item the Rule lists.

Crowell & Moring, on the effective date: "it will likely be several years before the impact of Rule 16.1 is clear" (Dec. 1, 2025). Sidley Austin, eleven weeks later: "[s]ince December 1, 2025, courts have taken varied approaches" (Feb. 19, 2026). Both quotations were checked against the source on 11 Aug 2026.

What the data shows

Half the judges cite the Rule. The other half cover about half of it.

Seven of the fourteen readable orders name Rule 16.1. Seven never do. All fourteen order a report and all fourteen take up leadership counsel, so the Rule is not being skipped outright. But the orders that cite it go much further: they hit about sixteen of the nineteen items tracked here, against about ten for the orders that don't. Two of the non-citing judges point counsel to the Manual for Complex Litigation instead. The likelier story is not that judges ignored Rule 16.1. It is that Rule 16.1 has not displaced the Manual.

Nineteen columns cover the Rule's eighteen listed items, because this dataset splits (b)(2)(A)(iii) into two: selecting leadership, and reviewing it periodically.

Leadership counsel is the one thing every judge addresses.

All fourteen orders take up leadership, including all seven that never cite the Rule. No other item comes close to that. The two least-addressed items are direct filing and helping the parties settle, at six of fourteen each.

Nobody has ordered disclosure of litigation funding. Not once.

Zero of fourteen. Rule 16.1 does not require the topic to be raised, and so far no judge has raised it. That is a clean zero, and it is the number the Advisory Committee's funding subcommittee is missing.

The wait for a first conference runs from three weeks to twenty-eight.

Judge Ross set one 21 days after transfer. Judge Baker set one 198 days after transfer. Rule 16.1 says the court "should schedule an initial management conference" and gives no deadline, so this is the Rule's openness measured in days.

Where the Rule is used, it travels as a form order.

Judge Quraishi's first order in the Dupixent MDL (D.N.J., June 2026) is the same form order Judge Bates entered in D.D.C. five months earlier, down to three added items that appear word for word in both and in neither the Rule nor its Committee Note. Two districts, five months apart. What spreads is not the Rule text. It is somebody's order.

Counting this by keyword search gives the wrong answer.

The first three orders looked at here all cited the Rule, which reads as 100% uptake. That was an accident of how they were found: a search for the phrase "Rule 16.1" can only return documents that use the phrase. Listing every MDL and reading every docket brought the number down to 50%. Any figure for this Rule's uptake built from a keyword search will be too high.

The one place the Rule actually requires something, it has already slipped.

Rule 16.1's only real requirement runs to the parties, not the court: they must meet and submit a report. In the Trans Union MDL, one group of plaintiffs filed its own Rule 16.1 report, alone, five weeks after the Rule took effect.

What Rule 16.1 says

Rule 16.1 is short and it is mostly optional. The judge should hold an early conference and should order the parties to file a report first. What that report must cover is the only hard requirement in the Rule, and it binds the parties, not the judge.

The report has to cover eighteen listed items: seven about leadership counsel, four more on scheduling, direct filing and related cases, and seven on the parties' early views about pleadings, discovery, motions, settlement and the main issues. The parties may raise anything else they want. Litigation funding is not on the list, which is why it shows up here as a zero rather than as a violation.

Read the full text of Rule 16.1

(a) Initial Management Conference. After the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation transfers actions, the transferee court should schedule an initial management conference to develop an initial plan for orderly pretrial activity in the MDL proceedings.

(b) Report for the Conference.

(1) Submitting a Report. The transferee court should order the parties to meet and to submit a report to the court before the conference.

(2) Required Content: the Parties' Views on Leadership Counsel and Other Matters. The report must address any matter the court designates—which may include any matter in Rule 16—and, unless the court orders otherwise, the parties' views on:

(A) whether leadership counsel should be appointed and, if so:

(i) the timing of the appointments;

(ii) the structure of leadership counsel;

(iii) the procedure for selecting leadership and whether the appointments should be reviewed periodically;

(iv) their responsibilities and authority in conducting pretrial activities and any role in facilitating resolution of the MDL proceedings;

(v) the proposed methods for regularly communicating with and reporting to the court and nonleadership counsel;

(vi) any limits on activity by nonleadership counsel; and

(vii) whether and when to establish a means for compensating leadership counsel;

(B) any previously entered scheduling or other orders that should be vacated or modified;

(C) a schedule for additional management conferences with the court;

(D) how to manage the direct filing of new actions in the MDL proceedings; and

(E) whether related actions have been—or are expected to—be filed in other courts, and whether to adopt methods for coordinating with them.

(3) Additional Required Content: the Parties' Initial Views on Various Matters. Unless the court orders otherwise, the report also must address the parties' initial views on:

(A) whether consolidated pleadings should be prepared;

(B) how and when the parties will exchange information about the factual bases for their claims and defenses;

(C) discovery, including any difficult issues that may arise;

(D) any likely pretrial motions;

(E) whether the court should consider any measures to facilitate resolving some or all actions before the court;

(F) whether any matters should be referred to a magistrate judge or a master; and

(G) the principal factual and legal issues likely to be presented.

(4) Permitted Content. The report may include any other matter that the parties wish to bring to the court's attention.

(c) Initial Management Order. After the conference, the court should enter an initial management order addressing the matters in Rule 16.1(b) and, in the court's discretion, any other matters. This order controls the course of the proceedings unless the court modifies it.

Quoted word for word, including the Rule's own punctuation. Text as published by the Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School, law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_16.1, retrieved 12 August 2026. Check it against the official text before quoting the Rule in a filing.

How this was built

Start with the Judicial Panel's own monthly list of MDLs, so the set is everything, not everything a search happens to turn up. Pull each judge's first management order. Read it. Record what it says about each item in the Rule, and write down where in the order it says so, by paragraph, so anyone can check the call.

Three habits do most of the work here:

  • Code what the order says, not what it seems to mean. Not a party's description of an order, not a firm's write-up of it, not the one-line docket entry.
  • Silence is silence. If an order simply doesn't mention something, that is recorded as "not addressed," never as a refusal. Only a judge who actually says no counts as a no.
  • When in doubt, leave it out. Anything unclear is recorded as unclear, with the language quoted rather than resolved. Every number here is a floor.

Every source links to a free copy, on CourtListener or on the court's own site. Nothing here needs a PACER account. If you think a call is wrong, say so and it gets logged in the changelog.

What this doesn't cover

  • Fourteen of sixteen MDLs. One order exists that hasn't been read yet: MDL 3187, where the docket has four orders and none of them is searchable text. One MDL, 3176, has issued no qualifying order at all, which is a fact about that case rather than a hole in the data. The two are never lumped together.
  • Six rows are thinner than the rest. For MDLs 3162, 3163, 3166, 3171, 3174 and 3175, the notes point to the right paragraph but don't quote it. The other eight quote the language directly. Those six were done first, before the standard tightened, and four of the seven citing orders are among them. They are being filled in.
  • First orders only, with one exception. A judge can pick the Rule up later even if the first order skipped it. One already has, in the Shell Eggs MDL.
  • Reports that never hit the docket. Two of the fourteen judges tell counsel to email the report to chambers instead of filing it, and one says outright that it "should not be filed." No method built on public dockets, including this one, can see those.
  • One person coded all of this, and nobody has checked it against a second reader. That is why every entry carries a paragraph cite: so an argument is with a quotation and not with a judgment call.
  • The Rule is eight months old and the numbers are small. Nothing here says anything about how this settles out in the long run.
