**Re: Suggestion Concerning Fed. R. Civ. P. 16.1 --- Empirical Data on
the Rule's First Eight Months, and Two Low-Cost Measures**

Dear Secretary Dubay:

I write to submit empirical data on how transferee courts and litigants
have applied Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 16.1 since it took effect
on December 1, 2025, and to suggest two measures the Advisory Committee
on Civil Rules may wish to consider. I am a third-year student at New
York University School of Law. I have no client, no funder, and no
interest in any of the litigation described below. The underlying
dataset is public, free, and offered to the Committee and its reporters
without restriction.

The short version: of the thirteen MDLs created since the effective date
whose initial management orders I have been able to read, **six cite
Rule 16.1 and seven do not**. No two of the thirteen orders take the
same approach. The interval between transfer and the initial management
conference ranges from 21 days to 198. Not one order has required
disclosure of third-party litigation funding. Two courts have directed
the parties' Rule 16.1(b)(1) report to a chambers email address rather
than the docket --- one instructing that it "should not be filed." And
the two Rule 16.1 reports I have been able to read each record a dispute
about what that report is supposed to be.

**I. The dataset and why the method matters**

Existing commentary on Rule 16.1 is uniformly qualitative. Firm alerts
report that courts have taken varied approaches without citing which
courts or counting how many. To my knowledge no one has assembled the
population.

I did so as follows. The universe is every MDL centralized by the
Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation on or after December 1, 2025,
taken from the Panel's own monthly *Pending MDL Dockets By MDL Number*
report (August 3, 2026 edition). That is sixteen MDLs. For each, I
located the transferee court's initial management order and coded it
against fifty-plus variables tracking every enumerated topic in Rule
16.1(b)(2) and (b)(3), the threshold applications of 16.1(a), (b)(1) and
(c), and matters courts added beyond the Rule. A second table records
party filings that invoke the Rule.

Three coding rules govern, and they are conservative by design. I code
only what a document says, never what it appears to mean. Silence is
coded NOT ADDRESSED, never NO; only an affirmative refusal is NO. Where
a document is ambiguous I code UNCLEAR and quote the language rather
than resolve it. Every affirmative finding carries a pin cite to the
document and paragraph. Where I was unsure whether something qualified,
I recorded that it did not. **Every number below is therefore a floor.**

One methodological point bears emphasis because it likely affects other
information reaching the Committee. My first three coded orders all
cited Rule 16.1 --- a 100% uptake rate. That was an artifact. Those
three surfaced from a full-text search for the phrase "Federal Rule of
Civil Procedure 16.1," and a full-text search can only return documents
that use the phrase. Only after enumerating the Panel's universe and
reviewing each docket individually did the rate fall to six of thirteen.
**Any measure of this Rule's uptake built from full-text search of court
documents will substantially overstate it**, and will be structurally
blind to precisely the orders that would be most informative to the
Committee. Several of the non-citing orders are also unreadable by
search because their PDFs carry no text layer at all.

**II. What the orders show**

**A. Six of thirteen orders cite the Rule; seven never mention it.** The
six are in MDL Nos. 3162, 3167, 3170, 3171, 3174 and 3175; the seven are
3163, 3166, 3172, 3178, 3179, 3181 and 3185. That is a fact about
citation, not about compliance, and I draw no inference from it about
any court. It is reported because it is the denominator everything else
sits on.

**B. Among the orders that do cite the Rule, no two use it the same
way.** Four distinguishable patterns emerged, and I record them
descriptively:

-   *The Rule as agenda.* MDL 3162 provides that the items listed in
    > Rule 16.1 "shall ... constitute a tentative agenda" for the
    > conference, reproduces (b)(2)(A)--(E) and (b)(3)(A)--(G), and adds
    > four topics of its own.

-   *The Rule as residual clause.* MDL 3171 sets out seventeen topics of
    > the court's own and then adds "any topics not already listed but
    > included in Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 16.1(b)."

-   *The Rule transcribed.* MDL 3174 and MDL 3170 reproduce the
    > enumerated topics using the Rule's own structure and add nothing.

-   *The Rule incorporated by reference.* MDL 3175 directs a joint
    > report "that addresses each of the matters listed in the rule,"
    > without enumeration; MDL 3167 requires "\[a\] Joint Case
    > Management Report addressing the matters included in Rule
    > 16.1(b)."

**C. The seven non-citing orders are not doing less; several are doing
more.** Every one of the thirteen orders addresses leadership counsel.
The single richest order in the set --- MDL 3163, which raises a
court-initiated case census, a plaintiff fact sheet, bellwethers, and
common-benefit compensation --- never mentions Rule 16.1. The clearest
analogue to the Rule's leadership-compensation item, 16.1(b)(2)(A)(vii),
appears in MDL 3172, which likewise never mentions the Rule. **A
citation count is a poor proxy for whether the Rule's substance is being
addressed**, which is why the dataset codes each enumerated topic
separately.

**D. Time from transfer to initial management conference ranges from 21
days to 198.** Rule 16.1(a) provides that the transferee court "should
schedule an initial management conference" and sets no time. The
observed intervals: 21, 29, 42, 48, 49, 61, 65, 84, 105, 126 and 198
days. This is the cleanest measure in the dataset --- one variable, no
coding judgment, trivially reproducible --- and it may be useful if the
Committee ever considers whether 16.1(a) should carry a presumptive
outer limit. I take no position on whether it should.

**E. Rule 16.1 has not displaced the Manual for Complex Litigation.**
Two orders designate the Manual as their governing framework --- MDL
3172 ("The Court will be guided by the Manual for Complex Litigation,
Fourth ... Counsel are directed to familiarize themselves with that
publication") and MDL 3181 ("Counsel are expected to be familiar with
the Manual for Complex Litigation, Fourth Edition"). Neither cites Rule
16.1. On the bar side, both Rule 16.1 reports I have read brief the
Manual, one of them citing six separate sections. Bench and bar, several
districts, independently. If that pattern holds, the more accurate
description of the first year is not that the Rule was overlooked but
that **it has not yet supplanted the authority practitioners and courts
already had**, which may be relevant to how the Rule is presented in the
Manual's next edition and in Federal Judicial Center materials.

**F. Three orders frame the MDL initial conference under Rule 16 rather
than 16.1.** MDL 3181 provides that the conference "will be held for the
purposes specified in Federal Rules of Civil Procedure 16(a), 16(b),
16(c), and 26(f)." MDL 3185 asks the parties to address "\[t\]he
scheduling of a Rule 16 conference, if necessary." MDL 3179 describes
the product as a "case management plan" and imports Rule 1's "just,
speedy, and inexpensive" formulation. I note these without inference;
Rule 16.1(a) is itself permissive, so nothing here is inconsistent with
the Rule.

**G. The Rule's number is becoming the name of a document.** This is the
clearest sign of the Rule taking hold, and a citation count scores it
wrong. Four instances across three MDLs, bench and bar: Judge Shelby's
order in MDL 3167 refers to the required filing as "the Rule 16.1(b)
Report"; the party filing in MDL 3170 is captioned "Fed. R. Civ. P. 16.1
Report"; Judge Peterson's leadership order in MDL 3175 credits an
applicant with "drafting the Rule 16.1 conference report on behalf of
plaintiffs"; and defendants in that MDL write "\[i\]n their Rule 16.1
report, the current parties proposed ...." In each, the Rule number is
not a citation to authority --- it is the *name of a filing*. A rule can
naturalize into practice as a noun before, or instead of, being invoked
as authority, and a binary "cites the Rule" variable cannot tell the two
apart.

**III. What the reports show**

Rule 16.1's operative requirements run to the parties' report, not to
the court. I have read two, and a third is known to exist and is not
readable. Both of the readable ones are on the public docket; both are
worth the Committee's attention; and **both record a dispute about what
the report is supposed to be.**

**The mechanism worked in MDL 3162.** On March 6, 2026, all plaintiffs
and all defendants filed a 49-page "Joint Initial Management Report,"
organized under seventeen headings tracking the court's order, recording
a February 2 meet-and-confer, and stating that "\[u\]nless otherwise
noted, the positions set forth below reflect the joint position of
undersigned counsel for the Parties. Where undersigned counsel for the
Parties could not reach agreement, the current positions of the Parties
are set forth separately under the labeled headings." That is the
Committee Note's design working as written.

**It did not work in MDL 3170.** There, two filings went in instead of
one: a one-paragraph joint status report and, the same day, a
thirteen-page unilateral report captioned "Fed. R. Civ. P. 16.1 Report."
Counsel filing the latter quoted the Note's single-report language and
stated that they "were unable to achieve this despite efforts to
confer." The same filing records a disagreement about how much the Rule
requires --- "other counsel may not agree that a robust approach to this
Report was necessary," and counsel "believed the truncated report did
not comply with the Court's \[order\]."

**The common thread is more interesting than either instance.** In MDL
3162 the joint report was produced, and the parties then divided over
whether meet-and-confer statements could be quoted in it. Plaintiffs'
counsel wrote that defendants "elected to incorporate alleged quotations
from meet and confer discussions to support Defendants' positions in
this Initial Joint Report," and that "doing so is antithetical to the
purpose and spirit of the meet-and-confer process." In MDL 3170 the
parties could not produce a joint report at all, and divided over how
much detail the Rule demands.

Rule 16.1(b)(1) and its Note establish that the report should be single
and may reflect divergent views. Neither says whether the report is a
*negotiation* document or an *advocacy* document --- whether positions
taken in the meet-and-confer are usable in it, and how complete an
answer to each enumerated topic is expected. Two of two readable reports
fought over precisely that. With N of two I generalize nothing; I flag
it because it is a discrete, answerable question that guidance could
resolve at no cost, and because it is invisible unless someone reads the
reports.

**The third report shows why that last point matters.** In MDL 3175 the
parties filed a Rule 16.1 report; it has no text layer in the free
archive and no copy exists on any of the roughly twenty member dockets.
Its content is known to me only because defendants later quoted it:
"\[i\]n their Rule 16.1 report, the current parties proposed that ...
any plaintiff whose action would be subject to transfer to this Court as
a tag-along action to the MDL may file directly in this MDL before this
Court." That is a substantive Rule 16.1(b)(2)(D) proposal, made in the
report and evidently adopted --- and it is legible only in secondhand
quotation.

**IV. Suggestion one: publish an official model Rule 16.1 order or
checklist**

Both orders that reproduce the Rule's enumerated topics in full depart
from the Rule text in exactly one particular, and the two departures are
different:

-   **MDL 3174** correctly introduces its second list with "Pursuant to
    > Rule 16.1(b)(3), the report shall also address the parties'
    > initial views on: (A)--(G)," and then introduces the permissive
    > catch-all with the same designation --- "Pursuant to Rule
    > 16.1(b)(3), the report may also include any other matters that the
    > parties wish to bring to the court's attention." The permissive
    > catch-all is Rule 16.1(b)(4).

-   **MDL 3170** reproduces seventeen of the Rule's eighteen enumerated
    > report topics --- all seven leadership sub-items of
    > (b)(2)(A)(i)--(vii), all four of (b)(2)(B)--(E), and six of the
    > seven in (b)(3) --- omitting only **16.1(b)(3)(E)**, measures to
    > facilitate resolving some or all of the actions. That order is
    > otherwise the most faithful implementation of the Rule in the
    > dataset.

The pattern is not confined to the bench. The brief discussed in Part VI
cites "Fed. R. Civ. P. 16.1(b)(3)" for the leadership provision, which
is 16.1(b)(2)(A). Two subsection mislabels, one on the bench and one at
the bar, in eight months, in a rule whose enumerated topics run to four
levels of subdivision.

I raise this in no critical spirit. The point is the opposite: **the two
courts that tried hardest to follow the Rule exactly are the two that
departed from it, because hand transcription is error-prone.** A judge
who wants to track Rule 16.1 closely has, at present, no authoritative
form to work from and must retype eighteen enumerated items.

There is also evidence that a gap propagates. In MDL 3170 the parties'
report tracks the court's order item for item --- and therefore contains
no section answering 16.1(b)(3)(E) either. The parties answered the
questions the order asked.

**Suggestion:** that the Committee consider recommending publication of
an official model Rule 16.1 initial management order, or a checklist of
the (b)(2)--(b)(3) topics, through the Federal Judicial Center, the
Manual for Complex Litigation, or the Committee's own materials. This
requires no rule amendment, costs essentially nothing, and would spare
transferee courts the transcription work altogether.

**V. Suggestion two: whether the Rule 16.1(b)(1) report should be
filed**

Two of the thirteen courts have directed the report --- or its
functional equivalent --- to a chambers email address rather than the
public docket:

-   **MDL 3163** directs the parties' position statements to a chambers
    > email address and provides that they "should not be filed."

-   **MDL 3172** provides that the leadership proposals and the
    > procedural-posture summaries "should be submitted only by email
    > to" chambers.

I want to be careful here. Nothing in Rule 16.1 requires the report to
be filed, these are sensible case-management choices, and I intend no
criticism of either court. But the consequence for the Committee is
concrete and, I think, not obvious: **where the report does not reach
the docket, no docket-based assessment of Rule 16.1 --- including this
one --- can observe what the Rule actually produced.** The orders remain
visible; the filings the Rule governs do not. At two of thirteen the
effect is modest. If the practice spreads, the Committee's ability to
evaluate its own rule degrades accordingly --- and, as Part III shows,
the reports are where the interesting information is.

**Suggestion:** that the Committee consider whether guidance --- in the
Manual, in Federal Judicial Center materials, or if warranted in a
future Committee Note --- should address whether the Rule 16.1(b)(1)
report is ordinarily to be filed on the docket, and note the
consequences for evaluation when it is not.

**VI. Rule 16.1 is being invoked outside Section 1407**

One finding sits outside the MDL population and I report it because it
would not otherwise reach the Committee. In *In re FedEx Tariff
Litigation*, No. 2:26-cv-02181 (W.D. Tenn.) --- a single-district
consolidation, not a Panel transfer --- counsel opposing competing
leadership motions briefed Rule 16.1 as persuasive authority, twice
acknowledging that it does not apply:

> *"Although new Rule 16.1, effective December 1, 2025, governs Section
> 1407 proceedings and so does not control this consolidation, its
> leadership instruction is squarely on point and reflects the
> considered judgment of the federal rulemakers ..."*

The brief then treats the Rule as interchangeable with existing
authority --- "\[w\]hether the source is Rule 23(g)(1)(B), the Duke
Guidelines, or the Rule 16.1 leadership note, the federal standard is
the same" --- and urges the court to follow "the \[course\] the federal
rulemakers have now modeled in Rule 16.1: to address the structure and
responsibilities of leadership at an initial management conference, on
the parties' submissions."

Two observations. First, if this recurs, **Rule 16.1 is beginning to
operate as a general standard for leadership appointment in aggregate
litigation, beyond the population it addresses** --- which no MDL-scoped
study would detect. Second, every substantive proposition in that brief
is sourced to the *Advisory Committee Note*, not to the Rule text. That
is unsurprising, since the Rule itself supplies no leadership-selection
criteria; but it means the operative content of Rule 16.1, as the bar is
using it, currently resides in the Note. The same is true of the MDL
3170 report, which quotes the Note four times and also cites written
testimony submitted to this Committee on the proposed Rule.

**VII. Data responsive to questions already before the Committee**

**Third-party litigation funding: zero of thirteen.** No order in the
dataset requires disclosure of third-party litigation funding, under the
Rule 16.1(b)(4) catch-all or otherwise. TPLF disclosure was deliberately
omitted from Rule 16.1, and the April 14, 2026 agenda book reflects the
funding subcommittee's continuing work. Whether courts would order
disclosure anyway under "any other matter" appears, so far, to be
answered in the negative in every post-effective-date MDL I can read. I
coded this variable in every row from the outset precisely so that the
denominator would be meaningful, and I will continue to report it.

**Census orders and plaintiff fact sheets: none ordered --- and where a
court asked, both sides said no.** Mandatory plaintiff-side vetting was
sought during rulemaking and not adopted. Across thirteen orders, one
court asked whether "an initial census or some similar process would be
beneficial" without ordering one (MDL 3162); one raised a
court-initiated census as a conference topic, stating that "\[t\]he
Court is interested in generating an initial census" (MDL 3163); and one
inverted the mechanism, directing the *defendant* to produce a case
inventory charting every member and tag-along action (MDL 3171). No
court has ordered a plaintiff fact sheet or census from plaintiffs in an
initial management order.

The answer in MDL 3162 is worth setting out, because it is the only
instance in which a court put the question to the parties and received a
considered answer. Section O of the joint report reads, in its entirety:
*"At this time, the Parties do not see the benefit or utility of
conducting a census of the current consolidated or future filed cases."*
That is a **joint** position of plaintiffs and defendants. Where a court
asked, the defense did not press for the mechanism the defense bar
sought in rulemaking.

That MDL is also the only one in which a court told the parties how to
operationalize 16.1(b)(3)(B), the contested factual-basis-exchange topic
--- and it operationalized it as a question about Rule 26(a)(1). The
order asks the parties to address "how and when the parties will
exchange information about the factual bases for their claims and
defenses, *including whether the parties should stipulate to dispense
with the initial disclosures required by Rule 26(a)(1) ... and, if not,
what if any changes should be made in the scope, form or timing of those
disclosures.*" The parties answered under a heading named for initial
disclosures. In the one MDL where the (b)(3)(B) question was actually
worked through, **it became a question about initial disclosures, not
about plaintiff fact sheets.**

**Leadership compensation is being addressed earlier than the Committee
Note contemplates.** The Note advises that compensation is ordinarily
best deferred until well into the proceedings. Four of thirteen orders
address it at the outset; the most explicit requires lead-counsel
applicants to state the "\[a\]ttorney fee proposals, rates, and/or
percentages that applicants expect to seek if the litigation succeeds in
creating a common fund" (MDL 3181). The MDL 3170 report takes the
opposite position --- plaintiffs' counsel argue the discussion is
"probably premature at this time" and should be "deferred until at least
a second case management conference," citing the Note. On this record
the front-loading appears to originate with courts rather than with the
bar.

**VIII. Limitations**

I state these plainly because the Committee should discount the data
accordingly.

-   **Coverage is thirteen of sixteen.** Two orders exist but I have not
    > read them: MDL 3180 (a free public copy has been located but not
    > yet reviewed) and MDL 3187 (four orders appear on the docket, none
    > available in the free RECAP archive). One MDL, No. 3176, has
    > issued no qualifying order at all --- a finding about that
    > proceeding, not a gap, and reported separately. I do not merge the
    > two categories.

-   **The report layer is N of two read, three known.** Nothing in Part
    > III supports a general claim. Both readable reports were read in
    > full and are quoted rather than characterized; the third is
    > described only as its own parties described it.

-   **One coder, no second-rater.** There is no inter-coder reliability
    > statistic. Every coding decision carries a pin cite so that any
    > disagreement is with a quotation rather than with my judgment.

-   **Initial orders only, with one exception.** I have coded the
    > initial management order in each MDL, not the full run of
    > subsequent case management orders. Later orders may invoke the
    > Rule where the first did not --- as one already has, in MDL 3175.

-   **N is small and the Rule is eight months old.** Nothing here
    > supports a claim about long-run practice. It supports a claim
    > about the first eight months, which is the only period there is.
